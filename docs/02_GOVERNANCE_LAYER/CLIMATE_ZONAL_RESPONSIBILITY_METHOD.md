## 1. Purpose

This document defines the Climate-Zonal Responsibility Method as a core governance method inside CCZPS 2.0.

The purpose is to move climate governance beyond national-border logic and toward climate-zone, ecological-function, and risk-exposure logic.

Traditional climate governance often assigns responsibility through:

- national emissions,
- GDP,
- industrial development stage,
- historical responsibility,
- legal jurisdiction.

These remain important, but they are not sufficient.

Climate systems do not follow political borders.

They follow:

- heat flows,
- water cycles,
- terrain gradients,
- wind corridors,
- vegetation belts,
- coastal exposure,
- dryland transition zones,
- human infrastructure pressure.

Therefore, CCZPS 2.0 introduces a climate-zonal responsibility method.

---

## 2. Core Definition

Climate-Zonal Responsibility means that climate governance responsibility, adaptation priority, ecological value, and development rights should be evaluated according to the functional role of each climate zone.

It asks:

> Where is this place located within the climate-ecological system?  
> What risks does it face?  
> What ecological services does it provide?  
> What development rights should it retain?  
> What responsibilities should it share?

---

## 3. Why National-Border Logic Is Not Enough

National borders are legal and political units.

Climate zones are physical and ecological units.

A single country may contain:

- coastal flood zones,
- dry inland zones,
- alpine water-source zones,
- agricultural plains,
- industrial heat corridors,
- forest carbon sinks,
- urban heat islands.

Each zone has different climate risks and different governance needs.

A national average can hide local vulnerability.

Therefore, climate governance must combine:

```text
National Responsibility
+
Climate-Zonal Responsibility
+
Community-Level Adaptation Priority
```

## 4. Main Climate-Zonal Categories

CCZPS may classify responsibility zones such as:

### 4.1 High-Hazard Zones

Examples:

- extreme heat zones,
- drought zones,
- cyclone or typhoon zones,
- flood-prone regions,
- wildfire-prone landscapes.

Governance priority:

- adaptation funding,
- risk reduction,
- emergency resilience,
- protective infrastructure.

### 4.2 Emission-Concentration Zones

Examples:

- industrial clusters,
- fossil-energy regions,
- transport corridors,
- high-density urban systems.

Governance priority:

- mitigation responsibility,
- technology transition,
- heat and emission monitoring,
- green industrial restructuring.

### 4.3 Carbon-Sink and Ecological-Service Zones

Examples:

- forests,
- wetlands,
- grasslands,
- alpine catchments,
- blue carbon coastal ecosystems.

Governance priority:

- ecological protection,
- RWA ecological valuation,
- long-term stewardship funding,
- biodiversity and water-source protection.

### 4.4 Resource-Transition Zones

Examples:

- coal regions,
- oil and gas regions,
- mining towns,
- old industrial districts,
- fossil-dependent export regions.

Governance priority:

- just transition,
- new energy development,
- workforce transition,
- green redevelopment rights.

### 4.5 Dryland Transformation Zones

Examples:

- semi-arid inland regions,
- desert margins,
- cold-arid belts,
- hot-arid belts,
- irrigation-dependent agricultural zones.

Governance priority:

- water-energy-ecology integration,
- evaporation control,
- soil restoration,
- climate-smart agriculture,
- ecological corridor design.

## 6. Relationship with Possibility Computing

Possibility Computing compares multiple future scenarios.

Climate-Zonal Responsibility provides the spatial responsibility logic for that comparison.

For example, a water-first scenario may be more suitable in a dryland transformation zone, while a blue-carbon scenario may be more suitable in a coastal wetland zone.

The same intervention should not be evaluated the same way in every climate zone.

Core principle:

> Different climate zones require different futures.

---

## 7. Relationship with TTEG

TTEG provides time-based governance logic.

Climate-Zonal Responsibility provides space-based governance logic.

Together:

```text
TTEG = When does the system become unstable?
Climate-Zonal Responsibility = Where does the system carry responsibility or vulnerability?
Possibility Computing = Which future should be selected?
```

This creates a complete governance structure of:

```text
Time + Space + Decision
```

## 9. Development Right and Ecological Duty

Climate-Zonal Responsibility should not be used only to assign burden.

It should also recognize rights.

A region may have:

- adaptation priority,
- ecological service right,
- carbon development right,
- green transition right,
- climate finance access right,
- community participation right.

This is especially important for:

- Global South regions,
- dryland communities,
- inland towns,
- Indigenous and traditional communities,
- resource-transition regions.

Climate justice requires both responsibility and development rights.

---

## 11. Human Governance Boundary

Climate-zonal classification should support governance, not determine it automatically.

AI may help classify zones and compare risks.

Human institutions must confirm responsibility, funding, rights, and implementation pathways.

Climate-zone logic should never become a new form of deterministic exclusion.

It should support fairness, not hierarchy.

---

## 12. Final Statement

Climate governance should not be blind to geography.

A fair climate future requires understanding where risks occur, where ecological services are generated, where development rights remain legitimate, and where responsibility should be shared.

The Climate-Zonal Responsibility Method gives CCZPS 2.0 its spatial justice logic.
